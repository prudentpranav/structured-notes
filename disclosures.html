Structured Note Disclosures & Risk Factors

2What are structured notes?

Structured notes are securities issued by financial institutions whose returns are based on, among other things, equity indexes, a single equity security, a basket of equity securities, exchange-traded funds, interest rates, commodities, and/or foreign currencies. Your return is “linked” to the performance of a reference asset or index.

Structured notes have a fixed maturity and combine two components: a bond component and an embedded derivative. Common types include principal protected notes, reverse convertible notes, enhanced participation or leveraged notes, income (contingent coupon) notes, and hybrid notes that combine several characteristics.

Structured notes are unsecured debt obligations of the issuer. They are not shares of the underlying asset, and holding a note does not give you ownership of, or the right to dividends or voting rights in, the underlying.

3Key risks

Complexity

You and your advisor should take the time to fully understand how the return on a note is calculated. The payoff may include leverage applied to the performance of the underlying, protection from losses if the underlying declines, conditions that must be met for coupons to be paid, early-redemption features, and embedded fees. Read the prospectus to understand how the note’s payoff incorporates the underlying.

Market risk and possible loss of principal

Some notes provide for repayment of principal at maturity, often called “principal protection.” Many notes do not offer this feature, or offer only partial protection. For notes without full principal protection, the performance of the underlying may cause you to lose some or all of your principal. Depending on the underlying, market risk may include changes in equity or commodity prices, interest rates, foreign exchange rates, and market volatility.

Principal protection depends on the issuer

Any principal protection is subject to the credit risk of the issuer. See “Credit risk of the issuer” below. Principal-protected notes are not bank deposits and are generally not FDIC-insured. Market-linked CDs may be eligible for FDIC insurance up to applicable limits; confirm eligibility and limits in the issuer’s disclosures.

Buffer versus barrier

Notes with partial protection generally use one of two designs, and the difference matters:

  • Hard protection (buffer). The note absorbs the first X% of any decline in the underlying. If the decline exceeds X%, you lose only the portion beyond X%.
  • Soft protection (barrier). You are protected only if the underlying does not fall below the barrier level (as measured under the note’s terms). If it does, protection is lost and you may bear the full decline from the starting level — not just the amount below the barrier.
Example (hypothetical): underlying declines 35%
20% buffer → loss of 15% (35% − 20%)
30% barrier → barrier breached → loss of 35%

Knock-in / barrier feature

If the underlying falls below a pre-specified level (a barrier, trigger, or knock-in) at the time or times specified in the note’s terms, you may lose some or all of your principal and may also lose coupon payments scheduled during the term. When the level is breached, the payout changes: instead of a return of principal you may receive an amount that reflects the decline in value of the underlying. For certain notes you may even receive the underlying asset itself, which will have declined in value.

Contingent coupons and memory features

On income notes, coupons are generally contingent: a coupon is paid only if the underlying is at or above a specified level on the relevant observation date. If it is not, that coupon is not paid, and you may receive little or no income. A “memory” feature, where present, may pay previously missed coupons if the underlying later recovers above the coupon level, but it does not guarantee that any coupon will be paid.

Participation rates, caps, and digital returns

A participation rate determines how much of an increase in the underlying is paid to you. For example, at 50% participation, a 20% increase in the underlying produces a 10% return. Rates above 100% amplify gains but do not apply to losses unless the note says so.

Some notes cap the maximum return; once the cap is reached you do not participate in further gains. Digital notes pay a fixed return if specified conditions are met, regardless of how much the underlying gained, and pay nothing extra for outperformance. Absolute-return features generally apply only within a stated protection range.

Call risk and reinvestment risk

Some notes can be redeemed before maturity. Autocallable notes are redeemed automatically if the underlying meets a specified condition on an observation date. Issuer callable notes may be redeemed at the issuer’s sole discretion, which the issuer is more likely to exercise when it benefits the issuer. If a note is called, you may not be able to reinvest your proceeds at the same rate of return, you stop receiving any further coupons or participation, and early redemption may occur at a time that is unfavorable to you.

Worst-of baskets, single stocks, and specialized underlyings

  • Worst-of baskets. When a note references several underlyings, the result is typically determined by the worst-performing one. Poor performance of a single underlying can determine the outcome even if the others perform well.
  • Single stocks and concentrated baskets. Notes linked to individual stocks or small baskets are generally more volatile than notes linked to broad indexes. Higher stated yields or participation typically reflect higher risk.
  • Sector, thematic, commodity, and digital-asset-linked exposures. These can be especially volatile and may be subject to risks specific to the sector or asset.
  • Proprietary, rules-based, or futures-based indices. Some indices apply fees, volatility controls, or synthetic dividend assumptions that can cause them to perform differently from the market they appear to track. Index methodologies can change.

Credit risk of the issuer

Structured notes are unsecured debt obligations of the issuer. The issuer’s promises to pay — including any principal protection and any coupons — are only as good as its financial health. If the issuer defaults or becomes subject to resolution proceedings, you may lose some or all of your principal and any other payments due, even if the underlying performs well. Changes in the issuer’s credit rating or credit spreads can also reduce the market value of the note before maturity.

Issue price versus estimated value

The price you pay at issuance will likely be higher than the fair value of the note on the issue date. Issuers disclose an estimated value on the cover page of the prospectus; it is typically lower than the issue price because the issue price includes costs for selling, structuring, and hedging the note. These embedded costs reduce the economic terms of the note compared with what might otherwise be offered.

Liquidity and secondary market

Notes (other than exchange-traded notes) are not listed on securities exchanges and typically have little or no secondary market. The only potential buyer may be the issuer’s broker-dealer affiliate or the distributor, and issuers often disclaim any obligation to repurchase or make a market. You should be prepared to hold a note to maturity; if you sell earlier you may receive significantly less than your principal, or may be unable to sell at all. Investors could lose a significant portion of their investment if principal is needed before maturity.

Valuation and daily pricing

Because most notes rarely trade after issuance, prices shown on account statements are usually calculated using models or matrix pricing rather than observable market prices. These values represent a best estimate by the pricing source, are not net asset values, and may differ from the price at which you could actually sell.

No dividends or ownership of the underlying

Returns on most notes are based on the price return of the underlying and do not include dividends. You will not have any rights in the underlying securities.

Conflicts of interest

The issuer and its affiliates may act in multiple capacities — such as hedging counterparty, calculation agent, and distributor — and may hold or trade the underlying assets. The issuer’s interests may differ from yours, and determinations made by the calculation agent could affect the amounts you receive.

Tax considerations

The tax treatment of structured notes is complicated and, in some cases, uncertain. Depending on the note, returns may be taxed as ordinary income rather than capital gains, income may be taxable before you receive cash (for example, imputed interest), and foreign tax consequences may apply. Read the tax disclosures in the prospectus and consult a qualified tax advisor before investing.

5Before investing in any structured note

  • Read the prospectus and offering documents in full, including the risk factors, the estimated value, and the tax disclosures.
  • Make sure you can explain, in your own words, how the payoff is calculated and what could cause a loss.
  • Understand the fees and costs, including the premium of the issue price above the issuer’s estimated value.
  • Consider whether a note is suitable given your investment objectives, financial circumstances, risk tolerance, time horizon, and liquidity needs — including whether you could need the money before maturity.
  • Understand any call features: what triggers a call, the earliest date it can occur, and how a call could limit your return.
  • Consider alternatives that may provide similar exposure with different return, fee, liquidity, and risk characteristics.
  • Review the financial condition and credit ratings of the issuer as carefully as you would for any other investment.
  • Consult your tax advisor about the consequences of a particular note.
Purchasing a structured note does not guarantee positive returns. The underlying may not increase in value — and even if it does, conditions in the note may limit your return. Interest rates, volatility, price movements, or other market variables may negatively affect your principal and performance. Investing involves risk, including loss of principal.
